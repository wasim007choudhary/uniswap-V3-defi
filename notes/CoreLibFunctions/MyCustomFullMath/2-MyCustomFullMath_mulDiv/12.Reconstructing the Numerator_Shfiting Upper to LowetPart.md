# 13. Reconstructing the Numerator — Shifting `upperPart` Into `lowerPart`

The next line is one of the most beautiful lines in the entire algorithm.

```solidity
lowerPart |= upperPart * twos;
```

At first glance, it seems completely unrelated to division.

We just divided the denominator.

We divided `lowerPart`.

Why are we suddenly multiplying `upperPart`?

The answer is:

> **Because dividing the 512-bit numerator by a power of two shifts bits from the upper 256 bits into the lower 256 bits.**

This line performs exactly that shift.

---

# Where Are We Right Now?

Originally our numerator looked like

```text
+----------------------+----------------------+
|      upperPart       |      lowerPart       |
+----------------------+----------------------+
         256 bits              256 bits
```

Together,

they represent

```text
Product

=

upperPart × 2²⁵⁶

+

lowerPart
```

---

# What Did We Just Do?

Earlier we found

```text
twos
```

which was the largest power of two dividing the denominator.

Suppose

```text
twos = 8
```

Then we divided

```text
lowerPart

↓

lowerPart / 8
```

Did we divide the whole numerator?

Not yet.

We only divided the lower half.

The upper half is still waiting.

---

# The Problem

Imagine a smaller example.

Instead of 512 bits,

pretend we have an 8-bit number.

Split it into

```text
+---------+---------+
| High    | Low     |
+---------+---------+
 4 bits     4 bits
```

Suppose the complete number is

```text
1011 1100
```

High

```text
1011
```

Low

```text
1100
```

Now divide the entire number by

```text
4
```

which is

```text
2².
```

Dividing by

```text
2²
```

means

```text
Shift everything right by 2 bits.
```

The result becomes

```text
0010 1111
```

Notice something.

The new lower half

```text
1111
```

contains bits from BOTH

```text
High

and

Low.
```

The bits crossed the boundary.

---

# This Is The Key Observation

Dividing the whole 512-bit number by

```text
2ⁿ
```

does **not** divide

```text
upperPart

and

lowerPart
```

independently.

Instead,

bits move from

```text
upperPart

↓

lowerPart.
```

That movement must be reconstructed manually.

---

# Why Multiply By `twos`?

Earlier,

we changed

```text
twos
```

from

```text
Largest power of two
```

into

```text
2²⁵⁶ / LargestPowerOfTwo
```

Example

If

```text
Largest power = 8
```

then

```text
twos

=

2²⁵⁶ / 8

=

2²⁵³
```

Multiplying

```text
upperPart × 2²⁵³
```

is equivalent to

```text
Shift upperPart left by 253 bits.
```

Those shifted bits land exactly where the missing bits belong inside

```text
lowerPart.
```

---

# Why OR (`|=`)?

The operator

```solidity
|=
```

means

```text
Bitwise OR and assign.
```

Earlier,

we already divided

```text
lowerPart
```

by

```text
twos.
```

So

```text
lowerPart
```

already contains the lower portion of the shifted result.

Now

```text
upperPart × twos
```

contains the missing upper bits that should slide into the empty positions.

These two bit patterns do not overlap.

One fills the lower positions.

The other fills the upper positions.

Therefore

```text
Bitwise OR
```

combines them perfectly.

---

# Child Analogy

Imagine two floors of a building.

```
Floor 2

[ A B C D ]

Floor 1

[ E F G H ]
```

Now imagine the entire building moves downward by

```text
2 rooms.
```

Some people from Floor 2 move into Floor 1.

After the move,

Floor 1 contains

```text
Some original people

+

Some people from Floor 2.
```

That is exactly what happens here.

The upper bits "move downstairs."

---

# Why Don't We Just Divide `upperPart` Too?

Many developers ask this.

Suppose we did

```solidity
upperPart /= twos;
lowerPart /= twos;
```

Would that work?

No.

Because the bits crossing from

```text
upperPart

↓

lowerPart
```

would disappear forever.

The relationship between the two halves would be broken.

The numerator would become incorrect.

The correct operation is

```text
Shift the entire 512-bit number.
```

Not

```text
Shift each half independently.
```

---

# Visual Flow

Before

```text
+----------------------+----------------------+
|      upperPart       |      lowerPart       |
+----------------------+----------------------+
```

Divide by

```text
2ⁿ
```

↓

Bits move across the boundary

↓

```text
+----------------------+----------------------+
|   Remaining Upper    |    New lowerPart     |
+----------------------+----------------------+
```

The line

```solidity
lowerPart |= upperPart * twos;
```

reconstructs this new

```text
lowerPart.
```

---

# Why Is `upperPart` No Longer Needed?

This is the beautiful part.

After this instruction,

every bit we still need has been moved into

```text
lowerPart.
```

Remember,

earlier we proved

```text
Result

<

2²⁵⁶.
```

That means

the final quotient fits inside a single uint256.

So once the useful bits have been shifted into

```text
lowerPart,
```

the remaining bits in

```text
upperPart
```

can never contribute to the final answer.

Their job is finished.

We no longer need them.

---

# The State Of The Algorithm

At this point,

we have achieved something remarkable.

Originally,

we had

```text
512-bit Numerator

÷

Even Denominator
```

Now we have

```text
256-bit Numerator

÷

Odd Denominator
```

The quotient has not changed.

But the problem has become dramatically simpler.

Everything is now ready for the modular inverse.

---

# Key Takeaways

* Dividing the 512-bit numerator by a power of two causes bits to move from `upperPart` into `lowerPart`.
* We cannot divide `upperPart` and `lowerPart` independently.
* Earlier, `twos` was transformed into `2²⁵⁶ / twos`.
* Multiplying `upperPart` by this new value shifts its remaining bits into the correct positions.
* `|=` merges those shifted bits with the already-divided `lowerPart`.
* After this line, all the bits needed for the final answer are contained in `lowerPart`.
* `upperPart` has completed its purpose and will never be used again.
