# 6. Recovering the Upper 256 Bits

At the end of `mul512()`, we have:

```solidity
low := mul(a, b)
mm := mulmod(a, b, not(0))
```

So we know two things.

First,

```text
low

=

Product mod 2²⁵⁶
```

Second,

```text
mm

=

Product mod (2²⁵⁶ − 1)
```

The only thing we don't know is

```text
high
```

the upper 256 bits.

---

# The Big Question

How can two remainders possibly tell us the missing upper half?

The answer comes from understanding how a 512-bit number is built.

Suppose the full product is

```text
Product
```

We can always write it as

```text
Product

=

high × 2²⁵⁶

+

low
```

This is not a trick.

It is simply how binary numbers work.

Exactly like decimal numbers.

---

# Decimal Analogy

Suppose you have

```text
123456
```

Split it into

```text
123

456
```

You can rebuild it by writing

```text
123 × 1000

+

456

=

123456
```

Notice

```text
1000
```

is simply the size of the lower block.

---

The exact same thing happens in binary.

Instead of multiplying by

```text
1000
```

we multiply by

```text
2²⁵⁶
```

because the lower block contains exactly

```text
256 bits.
```

Therefore

```text
Product

=

high × 2²⁵⁶

+

low
```

This identity is always true.

---

# Now Let's Look Modulo (2²⁵⁶ − 1)

We know

```text
mm

=

Product mod (2²⁵⁶ − 1)
```

Substitute the previous equation.

```text
mm

=

(high × 2²⁵⁶ + low)

mod (2²⁵⁶ − 1)
```

Now comes the beautiful trick.

---

# The Magic Identity

Modulo

```text
2²⁵⁶ − 1
```

we have

```text
2²⁵⁶ ≡ 1
```

Why?

Because

```text
2²⁵⁶

=

(2²⁵⁶ − 1)

+

1
```

When dividing by

```text
2²⁵⁶ − 1
```

the remainder is

```text
1.
```

Therefore

```text
2²⁵⁶ ≡ 1
```

inside this modulo.

---

Substitute that into the equation.

```text
high × 2²⁵⁶

↓

high × 1

↓

high
```

So

```text
mm

=

high + low

(mod 2²⁵⁶ − 1)
```

This is the key observation.

Instead of

```text
high × 2²⁵⁶
```

the modulo turns it into simply

```text
high.
```

---

# Rearranging

We now have

```text
mm

=

high + low
```

Move

```text
low
```

to the other side.

```text
high

=

mm − low
```

Looks done.

But there is one small problem.

---

# The Borrow Problem

Suppose

```text
mm = 5

low = 10
```

Then

```text
5 − 10
```

would become negative.

Unsigned integers cannot store negative numbers.

Instead,

they wrap around.

This means we need to subtract one extra borrow whenever

```text
mm < low.
```

The EVM checks this using

```solidity
lt(mm, low)
```

which returns

```text
1

if

mm < low
```

otherwise

```text
0.
```

---

# Final Formula

Putting everything together gives

```solidity
high := sub(sub(mm, low), lt(mm, low))
```

or mathematically

```text
high

=

mm

−

low

−

borrow
```

where

```text
borrow

=

1

if

mm < low

else

0
```

---

# Child Analogy

Suppose you owe

```text
10
```

rupees,

but only have

```text
5.
```

You borrow

```text
1
```

ten-rupee note from your friend.

Now you can pay.

That borrowed

```text
1
```

must be remembered.

The EVM does exactly the same thing.

Whenever

```text
mm

<

low
```

it borrows

```text
1
```

from the upper block.

---

# The Amazing Part

Notice what happened.

We never computed

```text
high
```

directly.

Instead we recovered it using

* one normal multiplication,
* one modular multiplication,
* and one subtraction.

No loops.

No large integer type.

No uint512.

Just mathematics.

---

# Complete Flow

```text
Multiply

↓

low = Product mod 2²⁵⁶

↓

mm = Product mod (2²⁵⁶ − 1)

↓

CRT relationship

↓

high = mm − low − borrow

↓

Full 512-bit product recovered

↓

[ high | low ]
```

---

# Key Takeaways

* Every 512-bit product can be written as:

  ```
  Product = high × 2²⁵⁶ + low
  ```

* Modulo `(2²⁵⁶ − 1)` turns `2²⁵⁶` into `1`.

* Therefore:

  ```
  mm = high + low
  ```

* Rearranging gives:

  ```
  high = mm − low
  ```

* If `mm < low`, an unsigned borrow occurs, so we subtract one extra.

* This leads to OpenZeppelin's elegant reconstruction formula:

  ```solidity
  high := sub(sub(mm, low), lt(mm, low))
  ```

* With only two `uint256` values, OpenZeppelin reconstructs the entire 512-bit multiplication.
